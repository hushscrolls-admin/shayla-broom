That night, Shayla dreamed she was a firewoman.

She had a red helmet, enormous boots and a shiny red fire engine.

She was checking her hose when a shout came from beyond the garden gate.

“SHAYLA! MY PYJAMAS ARE ON FIRE!”

Dad was standing on the garden table, holding a piece of toast.

Little orange flames flickered up his sleeves.

“What happened?” asked Shayla.

“I wanted my toast a tiny bit crispier.”

Shayla looked at the toast. It was completely black.

“How much crispier?”

Dad considered this.

“Possibly less crispy than this.”

Luckily, this was dream fire. It didn’t hurt Dad. But it tickled terribly, and every time he wriggled, another flame sprang up.

One popped out of his pyjama pocket.

“Oh, honestly,” said Dad. “I keep tissues in there!”

“Stay still,” said Shayla. “I’m coming!”

She pulled her hose from the fire engine and hurried through the gate. The gate swung back behind her.

Shayla planted her boots firmly, pointed the hose and squeezed.

A tiny trickle dribbled onto Dad’s slipper.

Dad looked down.

“Thank you. My big toe feels much safer.”

That wasn’t right.

Shayla checked the hose. It ran across the grass, round the bench—

And disappeared beneath the gate.

The gate had pinched it flat.

“Found it!”

She pushed the gate wide open and wedged it with a heavy flowerpot.

The hose swelled.

Shayla hurried back, gripped it with both hands and braced her boots.

“Ready, Dad?”

“Would it help if I said—”

The water rushed out.

“—NOOOOO?”

It hit Dad squarely in the tummy.

His pyjama top ballooned.

His toast flew out of his hand.

The flames on his sleeves vanished beneath a great sparkling splash.

“Good shot!” spluttered Dad.

“Thank you!”

“I think you got me!”

“I know!”

“I mean ALL of me!”

Water streamed down his trousers. It filled his slippers, overflowed his slippers and ran between the planks of the table.

Shayla eased the hose down.

Dad stood dripping.

“Excellent rescue,” he said. “Very thorough. I shall now go somewhere dry.”

“Wait!”

A little flame was dancing on the back of his pyjama bottoms.

“Turn around, Dad.”

“Why?”

“Your bottom’s still on fire.”

Dad turned so quickly that one slipper squelched off.

Shayla aimed.

Another great splash caught him from behind.

Dad shot up onto his tiptoes.

“Ooooh! That one went down my neck!”

The last flame disappeared.

Shayla checked his sleeves, his pockets and his soggy pyjama bottoms.

No flames.

Not one.

“Rescue complete!” she announced.

Dad climbed carefully down from the table.

Water poured out of his other slipper.

“Am I definitely out?” he asked.

“Definitely.”

“Even at the back?”

“Especially at the back.”

“Good,” said Dad. “Because I think I’ve swallowed enough water to grow a duck.”

Shayla laughed so hard that her helmet slipped over her eyes.

Dad lifted it gently.

“Thank you, Firewoman Shayla.”

“You’re welcome, Soggy Dad.”

Then he opened his arms for a cuddle.

“You’ll get me wet!” she said.

Dad looked at his dripping sleeves.

“Well,” he said, “somebody started it.”

***

Back in the bedroom, Dad woke with a surprise.

His pyjamas were wet.

The sheet was wet.

Even the bit of blanket he reached for was wet.

Shayla was fast asleep, cuddled against him.

There wasn’t a fire engine anywhere.

There had, however, been a very big wee.

“Oh,” whispered Dad.

He looked at his pyjamas.

Then at Shayla.

“Very thorough rescue.”

Later, when they were warm and dry, Dad asked her, “Were you dreaming you were a firewoman? Because somebody gave me a very good soaking.”

Shayla giggled.

“Was I putting you out?”

“Completely. You put out me, the sheet, the blanket and most of the bed.”

“I’m a good firewoman.”

“The best,” said Dad. “But next time, I’m wearing a raincoat.”

“And gum boots!”

“And a snorkel.”

Shayla snuggled into him, laughing.

Dad gave her a squeeze.

Then he checked his sleeve.

“Just making sure,” he said.
