Shayla and the
Lighthouse of Lost Songs

A seaside bedtime story

In the seaside town of Wrenhaven, every evening began the same way. The old lighthouse on the cliff would hum a low, golden song, and its beam would sweep across the harbour, guiding the fishing boats home. Shayla loved that song. She could hear it from her bedroom window, soft as a lullaby. But one blustery Tuesday, the lighthouse fell completely, eerily silent.

By morning, the whole town was muttering. The boats had struggled home through thick fog, and old Captain Merriweather had nearly scraped his hull on the rocks. "The lighthouse keeper has vanished," whispered the baker. "And the light won't shine without its song." Shayla frowned. Grown-ups were very good at worrying, she thought, but not always very good at doing something about it.

So after breakfast, Shayla packed her rucksack with essentials: a torch, a notebook, two apples, a ball of string, and her lucky blue scarf. She tucked a note beside her mum's teacup: "Gone to investigate. Back by dinner. Love, Shayla." Then she marched up the winding cliff path, the wind tugging at her scarf like an impatient puppy.

The lighthouse door creaked open on its own. Inside, a spiral staircase twisted upward into shadow, and the walls were lined with hundreds of tiny glass jars. Most were empty. But a few glowed faintly, and when Shayla held one to her ear, she heard a whisper of melody, like a seashell remembering the ocean. "Songs," she breathed. "Someone has been collecting songs."

At the very top, beside the enormous dark lamp, she found a round little creature no bigger than a teapot. It had silvery feathers, enormous anxious eyes, and it was sobbing into its own wing. "Oh dear, oh dear," it hiccupped. "I'm Pip, the keeper's songbird. Without the songs, the light goes out, and without the light, the boats get lost. And it's all my fault!"

Shayla sat down cross-legged and offered Pip half an apple. "Everything feels less terrible after a snack," she said kindly. "Now, tell me exactly what happened." Pip sniffled. A greedy storm-gull named Grizzle had swooped in during last night's gale, snatched the jars of songs, and carried the keeper off to his nest on Thunder Rock, a jagged island out beyond the harbour.

"Then we'll go and get them back," said Shayla, as if it were the most obvious thing in the world. Pip's feathers puffed up in alarm. "But Grizzle is enormous! And grumpy! And he hasn't said a single kind word in a hundred years!" Shayla tightened her scarf. "Then maybe," she said thoughtfully, "nobody has said a kind word to him either."

Down at the harbour, Captain Merriweather agreed to lend them his smallest rowing boat, the Dauntless Dumpling. Shayla rowed while Pip navigated from the bow, chirping directions. The waves grew choppy and grey. Halfway across, the fog rolled in so thickly that Shayla couldn't even see her own oars. "We're lost," wailed Pip. Shayla thought hard, then smiled.

"Pip, sing," she said. "Any song you know." Pip trembled, then began a small, wavering tune. Far away, through the fog, came an echo, bouncing back off something huge and rocky. "Sound travels and bounces," Shayla explained, steering toward it. "If we follow the echo, it'll lead us straight to Thunder Rock." Pip stared at her in awe. "You're terribly clever," he said.

Thunder Rock loomed out of the mist, all sharp edges and slippery seaweed. At the top sat an untidy mountain of a nest, built from fishing nets, old boots, and stolen umbrellas. The song jars were piled in the middle, glimmering. And tied up beside them, looking rather damp and very indignant, was the lighthouse keeper, Mrs Odalys Finch, still wearing her slippers.

Then came a terrible screech. Grizzle the storm-gull landed with a thump that shook the rock. He was as big as a wardrobe, with ragged grey feathers and a beak like a rusty anchor. "THIEVES!" he bellowed. "Those songs are MINE!" Pip dived behind Shayla's boots. Shayla's knees wobbled, but her voice didn't. "Why do you want them?" she asked.

Grizzle blinked. Nobody had ever asked him that before. His great shoulders slumped. "Every night," he muttered, "the lighthouse sings to the boats, the boats sing back, and the whole town sings together. But nobody ever sings to me. Out here it's only wind and waves. I thought... if I had the songs, I wouldn't feel so lonely." A single fat tear rolled down his beak.

Shayla stepped forward and gently patted his scratchy wing. "Stealing songs won't fix lonely," she said. "Songs only work when you share them. They're like hugs that way." Then she did something nobody expected. She sang. It was a silly, wobbly song she'd made up on the spot, all about a grumpy gull with very magnificent feathers. Grizzle's beak began to twitch. Then he laughed.

Grizzle untied Mrs Finch (who said "Well, really!" several times) and helped carry every single jar back to the boat. He even towed the Dauntless Dumpling home through the waves, flapping so hard that they skimmed across the water like a skipping stone. Pip sang the whole way, and this time his voice didn't wobble once.

That evening, Mrs Finch uncorked the jars one by one. The songs spiralled up into the great lamp, and with a whoosh of golden light, the lighthouse began to hum again. The boats sailed safely into harbour. And from then on, every night, the whole town of Wrenhaven turned toward Thunder Rock and sang one extra song: a song just for Grizzle.

Shayla arrived home exactly in time for dinner, with salty hair and a heart full to the brim. "Did you investigate?" asked her mum, smiling. "I did," said Shayla. "It turns out the lighthouse wasn't broken. Someone was just lonely." Later, tucked up in bed, she listened to the golden hum drift across the water, and far away a gull hummed along. The End.
